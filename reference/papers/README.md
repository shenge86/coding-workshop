# Papers

A short, curated reading list. The goal isn't to make you a researcher — it's to give you a *feel* for how the tools you're using actually came to exist, and why the recent leap surprised even the people building them.

You can skip this entirely. Nothing else in the workshop depends on it.

## Suggested reading order

The papers below are listed roughly in chronological order. Read in order to get the trajectory; jump in anywhere if you just want the highlights.

| Year | Paper | Why it matters |
|------|-------|----------------|
| 2017 | [Attention Is All You Need](attention-is-all-you-need.md) | The transformer architecture. Everything modern is downstream of this. |
| 2018 | [GPT-1: Improving Language Understanding by Generative Pre-Training](gpt-1.md) | Pretrain on a lot of text, fine-tune on a task. Sets the template. |
| 2019 | [GPT-2: Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners](gpt-2.md) | Scale starts to matter. Coherent paragraphs from a single model. |
| 2020 | [GPT-3: Language Models are Few-Shot Learners](gpt-3.md) | "Just give it examples in the prompt." Few-shot learning emerges. |
| 2023 | [GPT-4 Technical Report](gpt-4.md) | Multimodal, far more capable, far less told about it. The era we're in. |

Reading these back-to-back gives you a visceral sense of how fast things moved — and how recent the "AI that you can just talk to" era really is.

## How to read a paper without a PhD

- Skim the abstract first. If it doesn't grab you, skip the paper.
- Look at the figures. They usually carry most of the story.
- Read the introduction and the conclusion. Skip the methods and experiments unless you care about the details.
- Paste sections into AI and ask "explain this like I'm not a researcher." It will do a remarkably good job.
- Don't try to *implement* anything from a paper unless that's your project.
